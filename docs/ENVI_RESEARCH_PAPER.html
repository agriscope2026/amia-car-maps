Technical paper · draft for review

An El Niño Vulnerability Index for the Cordillera Administrative Region: method, exposure extension to high-value crops and livestock, and results for the October 2026 – March 2027 outlook

DA-RFO-CAR · AMIA Program October 2026 Reference dates: standing crops 30 Sep 2026 · livestock Q2 2026 · outlook issued Oct 2026

Abstract

The El Niño Vulnerability Index (ENVI) ranks the 77 municipalities of the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR) by how strongly an expected El Niño dry season threatens their agriculture. It combines three min–max normalized indicators with fixed weights: the PAGASA drought outlook (0.70), the historical El Niño damage to crops (0.15), and the agricultural exposure (0.15). This paper documents the method and extends the exposure indicator beyond rice and corn. High-value crops (HVC) are added as the standing area computed from 31,718 HVCDP planting records for the same reference date as the rice and corn inventory (30 September 2026). Livestock and poultry are added after converting 2.57 million head to 114,655 Tropical Livestock Units, normalizing them separately and blending them with the crop area (75 % crops, 25 % livestock), so that hectares and heads are never summed.

For the October 2026 – March 2027 outlook, every municipality outside Benguet is High (57) or Very High (6: Alfonso Lista, Flora, Tabuk City, Conner, Pinukpuk, Luna); the 14 Benguet areas are Very Low. The High and Very High areas hold 96,484 ha of the region's 112,858 ha of standing crops (85 %) and 75 % of its livestock units. This sharp split comes from the drought indicator: the season drought score varies only between 11 (Benguet) and 14 (all other provinces), becomes 0 or 1 after min–max scaling, and with a weight of 0.70 alone places every non-Benguet municipality in the High band. Damage and exposure decide the order within that band. Adding HVC and livestock changes the class of 2 municipalities and keeps the rank order close to the rice-and-corn index (Spearman ρ = 0.931). Rankings are stable under changes to the livestock share and the weights (ρ ≥ 0.95). With the drought outlook on a fixed scale (score ÷ 18) the same weights give 13 High and 64 Moderate municipalities, a more graded picture that we recommend for publication. We also show that most HVC standing at the end of September is harvested before the drought months, and we list the data gaps that most affect the result: Abra has no HVC records, Kalinga has no livestock figures, and a few very large HVC entries need to be checked.

Keywords: El Niño, drought, composite indicator, vulnerability index, exposure, high-value crops, Tropical Livestock Units, Cordillera, Philippines

1Introduction

El Niño episodes bring below-normal rainfall to northern Luzon, and in the Cordillera they translate into dry spells and drought during the dry season (roughly November to April). Rainfed rice and corn on the eastern slopes (Apayao, Kalinga, Ifugao) and irrigated vegetables in the Benguet highlands are hit in different ways, and livestock lose water and forage at the same time. The Department of Agriculture Regional Field Office (DA-RFO-CAR) needs a simple, repeatable way to decide where to pre-position seeds, pumps and water, where to concentrate field monitoring, and where to promote crop insurance before the drought starts.

The ENVI answers that need with a composite indicator at municipal level, updated every month when PAGASA issues a new outlook. It follows the common view of climate risk as the combination of a hazard, the exposure of people and assets to it, and their sensitivity (IPCC, 2014). Here the hazard is the drought outlook, exposure is what is in the field and in the pens, and past El Niño damage serves as an observed measure of sensitivity.

The original ENVI counted only rice and corn. This leaves out the high-value crops that dominate Benguet and parts of Ifugao and Mountain Province, and the livestock and poultry that matter in every province. This paper (i) documents the ENVI method as implemented in the CAR Agri-Climate Portal, (ii) describes how HVC and livestock were added without mixing incompatible units or reference dates, (iii) reports the results for the October 2026 – March 2027 outlook, and (iv) tests how sensitive the results are to the choices made.

2Study area and unit of analysis

CAR comprises six provinces (Abra, Apayao, Benguet, Ifugao, Kalinga and Mountain Province) and 77 municipalities, including the cities of Baguio and Tabuk. The unit of analysis is the municipality, using the regional municipal boundary layer (EPSG:4326). Inputs reported by province are attached to every municipality of that province. Names in the input files are matched to the boundary layer by exact match within the province, then by fuzzy matching (similarity ≥ 0.85), and finally by manual assignment in the validation step. All 77 municipalities were matched for every input used here.

4Methods

4.1Index structure

The ENVI of municipality i is a weighted sum of three indicators, each rescaled to 0–1 so that a higher value always means more vulnerable:

ENVI_i = w_D · D_i + w_H · H_i + w_E · E_i w_D + w_H + w_E = 1(1)

where D is the drought outlook, H the historical damage and E the exposure. DA-RFO-CAR set the weights to wD = 0.70, wH = 0.15 and wE = 0.15 (earlier issues used 0.50 / 0.25 / 0.25), so that the index follows the forecast closely: the drought outlook weighs more than twice as much as damage and exposure together. An area with any indicator missing gets “No data”.

4.2Normalization

Each raw indicator x is min–max normalized across the 77 municipalities (OECD/JRC, 2008):

x′_i = (x_i − min x) / (max x − min x) x′_i = 0 when max x = min x(2)

Min–max keeps the proportions between areas and is easy to reproduce in a spreadsheet, but the result depends on the extreme values of the issue: one very large value compresses all others toward zero, and an indicator with only two distinct values becomes 0 or 1 (Sections 6 and 7).

4.3Drought outlook

PAGASA categories are mapped to an ordinal score (not affected 0, dry condition 1, dry spell 2, drought 3) and summed over the six outlook months, giving a season score between 0 and 18. The season score is min–max normalized (Eq. 2). The portal also offers a fixed scale, score ÷ 18, which is compared in Section 6.

4.4Historical damage

For each municipality, the damaged area (ha) of each past El Niño event year is summed over crops and averaged over the event years (here 2019, 2024 and 2026; rice only in the available file). Municipalities that were never damaged score 0.

4.5Exposure

Crop area. Rice, corn and HVC are all measured in hectares of standing crop on the same reference date t (30 September 2026), so they are added:

A_i = rice_i(t) + corn_i(t) + HVC_i(t)(3)

HVC standing area. HVC_i(t) is computed from the planting records rather than from the crop-stage columns, using these rules:

  1. A record counts if it was planted on or before t (records planted later are excluded: 302 records, 1,040 ha).
  2. A short-cycle crop (vegetables, root crops, spices, cut flowers) counts while t is before its target harvest. Crops harvested many times (e.g. tomato, chayote) count for 63 more days, about nine weekly pickings.
  3. Tree and plantation crops (banana, coffee, cacao, mango, citrus, rambutan, dragon fruit and similar) count from planting on: their target harvest is the first harvest, not the end of the crop.
  4. Where the target harvest is missing or earlier than the planting date (575 records, 513 ha), the median crop duration of the same commodity in the file is used, or 90 days if there is none.
  5. Spellings of the same commodity are merged (e.g. Peanut, peanut, PEANUT), and a missing crop group is filled in from other records of the same commodity (3,551 records).

The same records give the area still in the field in each outlook month m: short-cycle plantings made up to t that are planted before the end of m and not fully harvested before its start, plus all tree crops.

Livestock. Head counts of different species cannot be compared directly: one carabao and one chicken would count the same. They are converted to Tropical Livestock Units (TLU), a standard measure of livestock biomass (Jahnke, 1982; Chilonda and Otte, 2006), using factors for Asia (Appendix B):

L_i = Σ_s heads_{i,s} × f_s f: carabao 0.70, cattle and horse 0.65, swine 0.25, goat and sheep 0.10, chicken and duck 0.01(4)

Combining crops and livestock. Hectares and livestock units are normalized separately and then blended with a livestock share s (default 0.25):

E_i = (1 − s) · A′_i + s · L′_i(5)

Without a livestock inventory, Ei = A′i, which is the original rice-and-corn ENVI. The weight of exposure in Eq. 1 does not change, so adding livestock re-divides the exposure share (0.15) rather than taking weight from the hazard or the damage.

4.6Classification

ENVI values are grouped into five classes of equal width: Very Low (0–0.2), Low (0.2–0.4), Moderate (0.4–0.6), High (0.6–0.8) and Very High (0.8–1.0), lower bound included. Equal intervals keep the class meaning the same from one monthly issue to the next; quantiles and natural breaks (Jenks, 1967) are available but not used here.

4.7Monthly ENVI

Each outlook month also gets its own map. Its drought part is that month's score divided by 3 (a fixed scale, so that a month with drought everywhere scores 1 everywhere instead of 0); damage and exposure are the same as in the season ENVI.

4.8Implementation and reproducibility

The method runs in the render worker of the CAR Agri-Climate Portal (worker/agriclimate/envi/; HVC and livestock in exposure.py). Every upload is validated (unit checks, name matching, data-quality warnings) before a draft is made. Each issue exports an Excel workbook whose Computation sheet repeats Eqs. 1–5 as live formulas, so a reviewer can change a weight or a value and see the classes recalculate. The large HVCDP workbook is reduced in the browser to the columns used; the reduced file gives results identical to the original workbook.

5Results

5.1Season ENVI, October 2026 – March 2027

With HVC and livestock included (scenario C), 6 municipalities are Very High, 57 High and 14 Very Low; the Moderate and Low classes are empty. The split follows the provinces. Benguet (13 municipalities and Baguio City) has the lowest season drought score (11), which normalizes to 0; its ENVI is at most 0.30 and all its areas are Very Low. Every other municipality has the highest score (14), which normalizes to 1 and contributes 0.70 to its ENVI on its own, already inside the High band (0.60–0.80). An area becomes Very High when its damage and exposure add at least 0.10 more, that is, when their normalized values average one third or more. The six Very High areas are Alfonso Lista, Flora, Tabuk City, Conner, Pinukpuk and Luna (Table 5).

Alfonso Lista ranks first because it has both the largest crop area (16,867 ha, of which 10,303 ha HVC) and the largest livestock count (9,997 TLU). Flora ranks second on the highest historical damage (664 ha a year). Together, the High and Very High areas hold 96,484 ha of standing crops (rice 44,003 ha, corn 34,050 ha, HVC 18,431 ha), 85 % of the region's 112,858 ha, and 86,438 TLU of livestock, 75 % of the regional total. Because almost the whole region is High, the ranking (Table 5 and Appendix A), not the class, is what separates priority areas in this issue.

5.2Effect of adding HVC and livestock

Three versions of the index were computed with the same drought and damage inputs: A, rice and corn only (the original ENVI); B, with HVC added to the crop area; and C, with HVC and livestock.

The class counts barely change (63 High or Very High in all three; Very High 8 in A, 6 in B and C) and the rank order stays close to A (Spearman ρ = 0.974 for B, 0.931 for C). The changes are concentrated where HVC and livestock are large (Table 6). Alfonso Lista rises from 6th to 1st, mostly from its plantation-crop records. Tabuk City drops from 1st to 3rd because its large rice and corn area is now compared against a larger maximum. Lamut climbs ten places on livestock. The Benguet vegetable municipalities gain a few places but stay Very Low because of the drought score. These ranks do not depend on the weights: changing 50/25/25 to 70/15/15 moves classes, not the order (Section 6.2).

5.3Monthly ENVI

Vulnerability builds through the season. In October no municipality is High (Benguet is Very Low and the rest mostly Low); in November and December 4 areas are High and most others Moderate; in January, when drought starts outside Benguet, 63 areas are High or Very High; and in February and March, when Benguet's outlook also reaches drought, all 77 are High or Very High. With the drought weight at 0.70 the monthly maps follow the forecast categories closely and show when each area enters the risk period, which the season map cannot.

5.4High-value crops and El Niño timing

On 30 September 2026, 32,472 ha of HVC were standing: 18,153 ha of short-cycle crops and 14,319 ha of tree and plantation crops. Ifugao (15,096 ha) and Benguet (14,020 ha) account for 90 % of it. The short-cycle area, however, empties quickly (Figure 3).

The ENVI's HVC term is therefore best read as a measure of how much HVC farming each municipality has, consistent with the rice and corn snapshot, rather than as a forecast of the area that will be hit.

The rank order hardly moves (ρ ≥ 0.988), but the class counts swing widely: from 6 High areas with equal weights, to 13 with the earlier 50/25/25, to 63 with any drought weight of 0.60 or more, and to 63 Very High at 0.80. The reason is the drought indicator. Because the season scores are only 11 or 14, D′ is exactly 0 for Benguet and 1 elsewhere, so the drought weight acts as a constant added to every non-Benguet municipality. With wD = 0.70 that constant alone reaches the High threshold. Under min–max scaling the adopted weights therefore make the classes describe the forecast (Benguet against the rest) and leave the differences between municipalities to the ranking, which should be published with every issue.

6.3Drought scaling

With the fixed scale (score ÷ 18), the non-Benguet provinces score 0.78 and Benguet 0.61 instead of 1 and 0. Under 70/15/15 the ENVI then ranges from 0.43 to 0.74: Benguet moves from Very Low to Moderate, 13 municipalities are High and the other 64 Moderate, with the same top five as under min–max (Figure 1). The fixed scale describes the outlook more faithfully (Benguet is only one step behind the other provinces, not free of drought), lets damage and exposure separate the High areas from the rest, and keeps the classes comparable between issues. It is the setting we recommend with the 70/15/15 weights.

6.4Very large HVC records

Forty-three HVC records of 200 ha or more (18,416 ha in total) look like municipal totals or existing plantations entered as single plantings, for example 2,000 ha of banana and 1,500 ha of mango in Alfonso Lista. Removing them lowers the HVC standing area from 32,472 ha to 23,532 ha. The ranking barely changes (ρ = 0.995), one more municipality becomes Very High (7 instead of 6), and Flora and Alfonso Lista swap first and second place (Table 6). The headline results are therefore robust to these records, but the position of Alfonso Lista depends on them and they should be verified with HVCDP.

7Discussion and limitations

A two-valued hazard with a large weight. The ENVI is meant to be driven by the drought outlook, and at 0.70 it is. But under min–max scaling the season outlook only separates Benguet from the rest, so the classes show two groups: Benguet Very Low, everyone else High or Very High. Within the five other provinces the order comes entirely from damage and exposure, which together carry 0.30. This is a property of the current outlook (five provinces share the same categories), not an error, but it means the published map says little beyond the forecast itself. The fixed scale (Section 6.3) or a finer hazard input, such as municipal rainfall as a percentage of normal, would let the hazard differentiate more and the classes carry more information.

Missing data counted as zero. Abra has no HVC records and Kalinga has no livestock figures. Both are counted as zero, which understates exposure in those provinces. Kalinga's livestock gap matters most: all eight Kalinga municipalities are High or Very High, and with their animals counted as zero their exposure under scenario C rests on crops alone, which keeps Tabuk City in 3rd place behind Alfonso Lista and Flora. Until the data arrive, results for these provinces should be read with this in mind. An alternative is to treat a province without any figures as “no data” for that component and renormalize the exposure from the remaining components.

What the HVC records represent. The HVCDP file records plantings that the program monitors. It is not a full inventory: older plantations that were never entered are missing, and some entries appear to be totals. Plantation crops make up 44 % of the standing HVC, mostly from a few large records.

Reference dates. Rice, corn and HVC share the same date by construction. The livestock inventory is from the second quarter (its title says March 2026); since herd sizes change slowly, a one- or two-quarter lag is acceptable, but the date should be confirmed and stated on every issue.

Exposure is a snapshot. The crop area is what stands on the reference date. For HVC this mostly measures farming activity, since the crops standing in September are harvested before the drought (Section 5.4). Rice and corn behave similarly for crops already near harvest. The exposure that matters in January–March depends on dry-season planting decisions that are not yet observed.

Skewed indicators. Damage, crop area and HVC are strongly right-skewed (Table 3). Under min–max, the largest value sets the scale for all others: Alfonso Lista's 16,867 ha puts most municipalities below 0.1 on exposure. A log transform or capping at a high percentile would spread the middle of the distribution.

What is not in the index. The ENVI has no adaptive-capacity component: irrigation service area, crop insurance coverage and access to water sources reduce vulnerability but are not counted. The portal already holds an irrigation layer that could be used. The historical damage covers rice only. The index has not yet been checked against observed damage; the 2026–2027 season offers the first chance to do so.

Weights are set by experts. The 70/15/15 weights reflect DA-RFO-CAR's judgement that the forecast should lead the index. The rankings are the same under every weighting tested (Section 6), which supports using the ranking for prioritization. The classes, in contrast, depend strongly on the weights together with the drought scaling, and should be treated as indicative.

8Recommendations

Data

  • Request Abra's HVC records and Kalinga's livestock figures; until then, flag both in the published issue.
  • Ask HVCDP to (a) check the 43 records of 200 ha or more, (b) fill the crop group and the target harvest date in every record, (c) use one spelling per commodity, and (d) provide dry-season planting targets by municipality, so that the monthly ENVI can follow the HVC actually in the field.
  • Obtain a standing inventory of plantation crops (bearing and non-bearing area) rather than relying on new-planting records.
  • Confirm the livestock inventory date and resolve the two checks the validation raised: Apayao's sheep subtotal and the identical hi-breed and native swine figures.
  • Add corn and HVC damage to the historical damage file.

Method

  • Use the fixed drought scale (score ÷ 18) for the season index with the 70/15/15 weights, so that the classes separate municipalities (13 High, 64 Moderate in this issue) instead of only provinces, Benguet's later drought is reflected, and classes are comparable between issues.
  • Publish the ranking (top 10 and full list) with every map; under min–max scaling it carries the differences the classes cannot show.
  • Confirm the livestock share of exposure (0.25 by default) and publish it with each issue.
  • Test a log or percentile-capped normalization for the skewed indicators.
  • Add an adaptive-capacity indicator (e.g. irrigated share of crop area) in a later version.
  • After the season, compare the ENVI classes with the damage actually reported, and adjust the weights if the comparison supports it.

9Conclusion

The ENVI gives DA-RFO-CAR a transparent, monthly, municipality-level ranking of El Niño vulnerability that can be recomputed and checked in a spreadsheet. Extending exposure to high-value crops and livestock makes the index cover the whole agricultural economy of the Cordillera without mixing units or dates: crop areas are added in hectares for one reference date, and livestock is converted to livestock units and blended after normalization. With the adopted 70/15/15 weights, the October 2026 – March 2027 outlook places every municipality outside Benguet in the High or Very High class, led by Alfonso Lista, Flora, Tabuk City, Conner, Pinukpuk and Luna. The ranking is robust to every choice tested; the class counts depend mainly on how the drought outlook is scaled, and the fixed scale gives a more informative map (13 High areas). Closing the data gaps in Abra and Kalinga and adopting the fixed or a finer hazard scale are the two steps that would most improve the next issues.
